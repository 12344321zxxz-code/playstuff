# Peoples, languages, land use and polities, 10,000 BC–2000 AD

## Methods and notation

This is a deliberately coarse calibration model rather than a claim that
the underlying quantities are directly observed. I divide the inhabited
world into twelve fixed macro-regions, anchor population and
cultivated-land totals mainly to HYDE and historical-demographic
reconstructions, anchor modern linguistic diversity to Glottolog, use
Seshat/Cliopatria and Taagepera-style polity reconstructions for
political scale, and then interpolate missing regional/date cells from
archaeological and historical evidence. HYDE 3.2 explicitly reconstructs
population and land use from 10,000 BC onwards; its central global
population estimate is about 4.4 million at 10,000 BC, with an extremely
broad plausible range, and it keeps cropland below 1% of global land
until the first millennium AD.
[\[1\]](https://doi.org/10.5194/ESSD-9-927-2017) Modern Glottolog lists
7,674 spoken L1 languages, grouped into 246 families plus 183 isolates.
[\[2\]](https://glottolog.org/glottolog/glottologinformation) A 2026
global Holocene language-diversity model estimates roughly 4,500–6,000
languages before domestication, followed by a much larger mid-Holocene
peak—possibly tens of thousands—before long-run contraction.
[\[3\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42490497/)

Every numerical cell below is `best[low–high]confidence`. `H/M/L` mean
high/medium/low confidence. **† means the number is a synthesis or
judgement made for this report rather than a value directly copied from
one source.** Almost all prehistoric polity and linguistic cells
therefore carry † even when their scale is source-constrained.
Percentages are of the relevant regional total unless stated otherwise.
“Claimed state/chiefdom land” is an **overlay** on the ecological-use
categories, so it is not meant to sum with farmed, pastoral, forager and
empty land. The latter four do sum approximately to 100%.

“State subject” is political incorporation rather than occupation: a
peasant living in Han China is counted as a state subject, not
separately as a farmer. “Farmers outside states”, “pastoralists” and
“foragers” therefore describe people outside substantial state
incorporation. “Chiefdom” is used broadly for territorially organised,
ranked non-state polities. Seshat's own methodology treats many polity
attributes as ranges and records uncertainty explicitly, while
Cliopatria now provides a worldwide geospatial catalogue of political
entities extending from the fourth millennium BC to the present; neither
is an exhaustive census of every small polity.
[\[4\]](https://seshat-db.com/methods/)

## World totals and the simulation-scale comparison

The first important calibration result is that the real world almost
certainly did **not** begin the Holocene as anything resembling eleven
linguistically homogeneous founding bands. Even with only several
million humans, the best current language-diversity modelling points to
several thousand languages at 10,000 BC, which implies a very large
number of small ethnolinguistic communities.
[\[3\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42490497/) The simulation
pattern “one family fills a whole continent, but fragments into many
states” does have historical analogues after large demic
expansions—Indo-European Europe, Bantu-speaking much of sub-equatorial
Africa, Austronesian island worlds—but it is much less representative of
initial settlement or of continents that retained several old population
strata. Diamond and Bellwood's classic synthesis likewise treats
farming/language dispersals as some of the largest population expansions
and replacements since the Ice Age, not as the primordial condition
everywhere. [\[5\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12714734/)

HYDE's older central series gives about 2 million people at 10,000 BC
and 6.145 billion in 2000, while HYDE 3.2 enlarged the early estimate to
about 4.4 million and explicitly gives a 10,000 BC uncertainty envelope
stretching from effectively near zero to 8.9 million.
[\[8\]](https://geo.public.data.uu.nl/vault-hyde-data/HYDE%203.1%5B1655214378%5D/original/Klein_Goldewijk_et_al_2011-GEB.pdf)
Historical estimates around 1 AD are especially dispersed—roughly
170–400 million across major reconstructions—so the range here is
intentionally wide.
[\[9\]](https://cdn.www.census.gov/data/tables/time-series/demo/international-programs/historical-est-worldpop.html)

The language curve is much less secure than population. The unusually
high 1000 BC–1 AD figures are intentional: recent Holocene modelling
suggests that linguistic diversity probably rose substantially as
agriculture raised population density without immediately producing
giant language communities, then fell as large states and expanding
languages absorbed smaller ones.
[\[3\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42490497/) For comparison,
D-PLACE's Ethnographic Atlas database contains 1,291 sampled societies,
mostly documented in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries; that
is a sample of ethnographic units, not a census of all peoples.
[\[10\]](https://d-place.org/about)

Taagepera's macro-political reconstruction reaches the same qualitative
conclusion from another direction: the effective number of political
units fell by orders of magnitude over the long run, with major upward
jumps in maximum polity size around the rise of the first states, the
first great territorial empires and the early-modern imperial age.
[\[11\]](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/8vx325vq) The raw counts above
are much larger than “effective number” because they attempt to include
small chiefdoms and states.

### How often were many states from the same language family?

At continental scale the answer is **frequently, but rarely
exclusively**. Once a successful language family had undergone a large
demographic expansion, political fragmentation could yield dozens or
hundreds of independent polities whose languages were related. The
clearest cases include Indo-European-speaking Europe, Greek poleis,
Germanic polities, much of Bantu-speaking Africa, numerous Austronesian
island polities and Chinese states within the Sinitic sphere.
Conversely, South Asia, the steppe, much of Southeast Asia, the Americas
and many imperial borderlands retained several unrelated families side
by side. The matrix below gives a quantitative proxy: `commonfam%` is
the percentage of independent states + chiefdoms estimated to belong to
the single most common family in that macro-region.

A useful simulation rule is therefore: **large farmer expansion →
temporarily low family diversity but high polity count**. Political
unification is not the automatic consequence of linguistic kinship. What
is unusual in the proposed simulation is not “many same-family states”;
it is producing that pattern continent-wide from only a handful of
founders and maintaining too much unoccupied land after populations have
become large.

## Farmer and language-front expansions

The decisive historical mechanism for producing “one family over a huge
area but many separate polities” is not initial colonisation by a tiny
number of surviving bands; it is **later demographic expansion of
farmers or pastoralists through an already inhabited landscape**.
Archaeological, linguistic and ancient-DNA evidence generally points to
mixtures of migration, population growth, intermarriage and cultural
adoption rather than one universal replacement process.
[\[13\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12714734/)

The European Neolithic figure has unusually good quantitative support:
radiocarbon-based studies repeatedly put the average front at about
0.6–1.3 km/year, roughly 60–130 km per century, with a central value
near 1 km/year.
[\[14\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1287502/)

For Bantu, contemporary phylogeographic work notes more than 500 Bantu
languages spoken over roughly 9 million km² today, while modelling gives
eastward speeds around 0.59–1.27 km/year and southern rates around
1.5–2.27 km/year.
[\[15\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9372543/) The ancestry
signal is broadly consistent with demic expansion plus varying local
incorporation rather than clean extermination.
[\[16\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6506142/)

Austronesian expansion illustrates why simple “kilometres per century”
can mislead for maritime dispersals. Archaeological-linguistic sequences
place movement from Taiwan through the Philippines towards Sulawesi and
coastal New Guinea mainly between about 3000 and 2000 BC; subsequently,
Lapita-associated settlement covered the Admiralties-to-Samoa span in
only about 300 years, an average straight-line pace near 13 km/year.
[\[17\]](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/p63701/html/ch02s05.html)

The steppe/Indo-European event was similarly episodic rather than a
smooth wave. The Yamnaya archaeological complex appears around 3300 BC
and reached a Hungary-to-Kazakhstan range by about 3000 BC; genetically
related populations covered an east–west span of roughly 5,000 km during
the 3000–2500 BC interval.
[\[18\]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/indoeuropean-puzzle-revisited/yamnaya-culture-and-the-invention-of-nomadic-pastoralism-in-the-eurasian-steppes/9C6CA3B54CDC8045A105B7084CB453F4)
Corded Ware populations in parts of central Europe carried very high
proportions of steppe ancestry—one influential analysis estimated about
75% in the sampled German Corded Ware group—so this is one of the
strongest examples of substantial prehistoric demographic replacement.
[\[19\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature14317)

Arabic and Turkic show the opposite extreme: military-political fronts
could move vastly faster than language and genes. Arabic armies expanded
from Arabia across the Levant, North Africa and beyond during the
seventh and early eighth centuries, but Arabicisation of much of North
Africa was a centuries-long process and intensified further after later
medieval migrations; ancient and modern genetic evidence shows
considerable continuity of earlier North African populations.
[\[20\]](https://openstax.org/books/world-history-volume-1/pages/11-2-the-arab-islamic-conquests-and-the-first-islamic-states)
Turkic-speaking populations likewise show highly variable East Eurasian
ancestry, and Anatolian Turkish populations are predominantly descended
from pre-Turkic Anatolians with a minority Central Asian contribution
rather than from wholesale replacement.
[\[21\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pgen.1005068)

## Multi-ethnic states and political fragmentation

For this table, a “minority-ruler state” means a state in which the
language of the ruling stratum was spoken natively by **less than half**
its subjects. This is not the same thing as an ethnically diverse state:
Han China, for example, could be highly diverse yet still have a
majority population belonging to the ruler's broad linguistic-cultural
group. Colonial empires, conquest dynasties and many steppe empires push
this measure sharply upward.

The increase to 1900 reflects the enormous territorial reach of European
colonial empires, the Russian Empire, Qing frontier domains and other
multi-ethnic imperial systems; the sharp decline by 2000 reflects
decolonisation and the multiplication of formally national states,
though many modern states remain multilingual. This is a judgement-based
transformation of political geography rather than a published global
time series. Cliopatria and Seshat provide the polity geography and size
scaffolding from which such a reconstruction can be made.
[\[22\]](https://seshat-db.com/core/cliopatria/)

For game calibration, the relationship between language family and state
count is better thought of as a **U-shaped political process**. A newly
expanding farmer family can generate dozens or hundreds of daughter
communities; those communities become chiefdoms and small states; a few
then form large kingdoms or empires; fragmentation later restores
multiple states without recreating the old linguistic diversity. China
is the classic example of repeated political fragmentation within a
strongly Sinitic core, while Europe is the classic case in which one
very broad family—Indo-European—remained politically fragmented for
millennia.

A reasonable rule of thumb from the matrix is that a large historical
macro-region at its political-fragmentation peak commonly contains
**tens of states and hundreds of chiefdoms**, not merely a handful of
countries. Conversely, the three largest polities often hold less than
one-third of regional population before full imperial consolidation.
Seshat examples show how quickly individual polity populations could
nevertheless become large: its coded ranges include roughly 1–1.5
million people for Old Kingdom Egypt and around 13.5 million for Western
Zhou China.
[\[23\]](https://seshat-db.com/sc/polity_populations_all/?orderby=year_from&per_page=200)

The **least trustworthy individual cells** are the prehistoric regional
language and ethnolinguistic-population estimates, especially the very
high inferred language counts in Sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia,
South America and the Pacific between roughly 3000 BC and 1000 AD. They
are deliberately broad extrapolations from the 2026 Holocene diversity
model rather than counts recoverable from named languages.
[\[3\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42490497/) The next least secure
are prehistoric chiefdom counts and “claimed territory”, because Seshat
and related databases are structured samples of known polities rather
than censuses of every local political community.
[\[24\]](https://seshat-db.com/methods/)

The **most robust calibration implications** are considerably firmer
than any one cell: human settlement began the Holocene with thousands
rather than a dozen linguistic communities; farming expansions sometimes
spread related language families across millions of square kilometres;
those related peoples routinely remained divided among many independent
polities; political territory expanded much faster than intensively
occupied land; and linguistic homogenisation generally required
demographic expansion, state formation and repeated language shift over
millennia rather than simply the initial peopling of a continent.
[\[25\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12714734/)
